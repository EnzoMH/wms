<img src="https://www.dcvelocity.com/media-library/illustration-of-a-pallet-with-boxes-on-it.jpg?id=61700813&amp;width=1200&amp;height=600&amp;coordinates=0%2C36%2C0%2C37" /><br /><br /><h3></h3><br /><p>Building pallet loads may just be the toughest job in the warehouse—but it’s about to get easier.</p><p>Mature technologies that have been helping manufacturers build better pallets for years are now finding their way to the floors of distribution centers to tackle burdensome inbound logistics tasks, for example. And new technologies are helping high-volume fulfillment centers build smarter, safer pallet loads during the picking process. In both cases, the aim is to ease labor constraints, improve throughput, and keep costs in line.</p><p>Matt Kohler, director of applications at material handling systems integrator <a href="https://www.bastiansolutions.com/" target="_blank">Bastian Solutions</a>, points to the heavy lifting involved in building pallets combined with a growing scarcity of warehouse labor as key drivers of demand.</p><p>“[Building pallets] is definitely one of the harder jobs in the warehouse,” he says, emphasizing the physical demands of lifting and stacking boxes throughout a shift. “But it was always an easy problem to throw labor at. Now, as labor has gotten scarcer, [palletizing technology] has [attracted] increasing interest [from] customers.”</p><p>To that end, software solutions are helping companies get a better handle on palletizing throughout the warehouse—from the loading dock to the warehouse aisles.</p><h3>​EASING INBOUND LOGISTICS</h3><br /><p>Palletizing is the process of planning and optimizing how products are placed, stored, and transported on pallets—which helps increase storage density, efficiency, and the speed at which goods move through the warehouse, <a href="https://maveneer.com/palletizing" target="_blank">according to information</a> from systems integrator <a href="https://maveneer.com/" target="_blank">Maveneer</a>. The process becomes more complex the greater the number of stock-keeping units (SKUs) you have and the higher the volume your operation handles—and that’s where software and technology can help.</p><p>Kohler points to floor-loaded containers that arrive at the loading docks of warehouses and fulfillment centers nationwide as a prime example. Overseas imports are often floor loaded for optimal container utilization, with boxes or cases stacked loosely from floor to ceiling, he explains. Warehouse associates must then unload the boxes and organize them for storage and retrieval in the facility. Palletizing makes for easy putaway and storage, but the process is arduous if done manually. Robotic palletizers that are programmed to optimally stack all those boxes can help speed up the process and ease the physical burden on associates.</p><p>High-throughput manufacturing organizations have been using such robotic palletizing hardware and software in their production operations for years, and Kohler says it’s now gaining traction in the warehouse as more companies seek to automate their logistics operations. He points to rising demand for Bastian’s <a href="https://brand.bastiansolutions.com/original/gallery/13887/files/original/d69b4d2d-705c-4b8c-bdf0-86fc34de9ca6.pdf?_gl=1*1msvdb*_gcl_au*MTcxMTM4MTYzOS4xNzU2MDU0NDg4*_ga*Mjc2OTQ1NjIzLjE3NTYwNTQ0ODg.*_ga_00JL13P2NN*czE3NTYwNTQ0ODckbzEkZzAkdDE3NTYwNTQ0ODckajYwJGwwJGg2NTM1MTE5ODg." target="_blank">StackOrder</a> platform as an example. The solution combines industrial or collaborative robots, dynamic pallet pattern software, end-of-arm tooling, and seamless integration with other software and equipment in the warehouse to improve palletizing—and it’s particularly useful on the inbound side, working to palletize all those floor-loaded cases.</p><p>As Kohler describes it, the software tells the robots how to build pallets based on the dimensions of the boxes in the trailer, using hundreds of pre-coded pallet patterns for the various products coming into the facility. The software is a key differentiator, he says, because it takes a new approach to encoding patterns that offers a high level of flexibility.</p><p>“Many [software] solutions on the market generate and store fixed coordinates for each case on a pallet,” he explains, noting that, by contrast, StackOrder “stores a relationship map, allowing a single pattern to ‘stretch’ with varying case sizes.” Kohler goes on to say that Bastian’s “most recent implementation handled about 9,000 SKUs ranging in size from about 9 by 8 by 3 inches up to 30 by 22 by 9 inches with about 200 unique patterns” and notes that “the built-in editor will allow customers to make new maps and arrangements for cases in the future.”</p><p>The cases are transferred to a robotic palletizer positioned outside of the trailer. Once the pallet load is built, the pallet is conveyed to storage, usually by an automated forklift or similar piece of equipment.<strong></strong></p><p>Speed and safety are the biggest benefits.</p><p>“There is a large improvement in ergonomics, and [you get] significantly higher throughput as well,” Kohler explains, emphasizing the labor savings in particular. “[The process] eliminates an incredible amount of walking and sortation—there are zero touches [from the time an item] comes out of the trailer to [the time it’s] put away.</p><p>“With labor shortages and labor cost increases—and the continual rise in the amount of goods that come from overseas that are [floor loaded]—there is definitely more interest [in this technology].”</p><h3>​PICKING THE PROPER PALLET</h3><br /><p>Building efficient, safe pallet loads becomes even more challenging on the outbound side of high-volume warehousing and fulfillment operations—primarily because of the wide variety of SKUs being picked and palletized. Mixed-SKU pallets must be built in a way that avoids product damage and reduces the risk of injury to workers—making sure the heaviest items are on the bottom, for example, and that odd-shaped items fit within the pallet’s dimensions. Demand for customized pallets adds another layer of complexity: Some retail customers request that pallets be built so that store employees can easily put items away based on the store layout, for example.</p><p>These issues contribute to three main challenges when it comes to building pallet loads, according to Ken Ramoutar, chief marketing officer at distribution center technology provider <a href="https://www.lucasware.com/" target="_blank">Lucas Systems</a>:</p><ul><li>The first challenge is the high cost of a bad or damaged pallet. Ramoutar says industry estimates show that, on average, 2% of pallets arrive at customer locations damaged, primarily due to compression. When that happens, costs add up in the form of returns, chargebacks, and customer dissatisfaction.</li><li>The second problem is that pallet building requires expertise: “Traditionally in picking, it’s up to the picker to assemble a good pallet while picking,” Ramoutar explains. “This takes a lot of experience, so new workers often build poor pallets—or the pallets have to be rebuilt in a separate operation before being wrapped and loaded.”</li><li>The third problem is that manual pallet building is inefficient: “Even experienced workers will often partially rebuild a pallet while picking as they see the sequence of picks unfold,” Ramoutar says.</li></ul><p>Software solutions can help address these challenges—especially solutions that are designed to guide associates through a process that ensures each pallet load is built in the correct order, with the right items in the right positions. Lucas Systems released its <a href="https://www.lucasware.com/dynamic-pallet-builder/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Dynamic Pallet Builder</a> software earlier this year to address those very issues. The voice-directed software guides warehouse workers while they are picking, directing them through the most efficient paths and sequences to build pallet loads that are properly structured, safe, and meet customer specifications. The artificial intelligence (AI)-powered software is dynamic, meaning it constantly recalculates the best pallet build as orders come into the warehouse.</p><p>Ramoutar says AI has been a game-changer in developing such advanced solutions. Dynamic Pallet Builder integrates with a company’s warehouse management system (WMS) or other warehouse inventory management platform, learning as it receives more data so that it can make better choices in guiding the pick and building the pallet load. Essentially, the software analyzes all of the products in the warehouse, their locations, what’s on the order, how much travel is required, and the distance between products, among other criteria, when sequencing a pick—all while recalculating as new orders flow into the warehouse throughout the day.</p><p>“It would be virtually impossible for a human to calculate how to build a good pallet” based on those factors, Ramoutar says. “We don’t just decide how the pallet should be built in the morning. [The system is] constantly optimizing.”</p><p>He says balancing all those factors makes building the perfect pallet load a “meaty problem.”</p><p>“On the surface, this is a Rubik’s Cube type of problem—but it’s actually much bigger than that,” Ramoutar says. “Every customer may have different requirements, so you can see how this gets hard very fast.”</p><p>Automation can help make solving that puzzle easier—which has impacts on both the cost and revenue side of the equation, he says. Using software not only makes the process more efficient but also helps the user better meet its customers’ ever-evolving demands, which ultimately provides it with a competitive advantage.</p>

That tool, the <a href="https://www.manh.com/solutions/manhattan-active-platform/agentic-ai-in-manhattan-solutions#:~:text=Manhattan%20Agent%20Foundry%E2%84%A2" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">“Manhattan Agent Foundry,”</a> is the same platform that the software developer’s research and development (R&amp;D) team uses to build the commercial AI agents the company offers but has now been opened up for clients to either use themselves or contract with Manhattan or third-party partners to develop new specialized agents.</p><p>According to Siotia, such AI agents could be used in a variety of common supply chain applications, such as:</p><ul><li>Managing warehouse flows, handling tasks like balancing inventory reserves, identifying tasks that are running behind schedule, determining whether reassigning warehouse workers would get operations back on track, and then communicating to those workers exactly where they should move.</li><li>Transportation invoicing tasks, such as assigning AI agents to read the PDF files that are frequently emailed by small carriers and then entering that information into a shipper’s software platform and taking steps to solve any problems that are revealed.</li><li>Fulfillment problem resolution, like answering “Where’s my stuff?” questions and tracking down missing inbound shipments.</li></ul><h3>​HOW TO TRAIN YOUR NEW AI ASSISTANT</h3><br /><p>When it comes to getting the most from AI agents, proper training is the key, says Rachit Lohani, chief product and technology officer at <a href="https://www.e2open.com/" target="_blank">supply chain software developer e2open</a>. In Lohani’s view, a common mistake in the market is to conflate automation—which is deterministic and role-based, with zero intelligence—with AI, which functions more like an aide or associate.</p><p>“Agentic AI is about providing you with an assistant. So [to design it], we have to understand your key metrics, how you make decisions, how you rationalize, and what you are optimizing for. Then when we train the model, we tell it, ‘Start making decisions like that,’ and from there, it keeps learning,” Lohani says.</p><p>A hallmark of AI technology is that it gets better over time, internalizing lessons on how to improve its performance every time a human mentor corrects it. And that means that supply chain professionals play a critical role in providing oversight and feedback to the agentic AI systems, he says.</p><p>“We understand the value of ‘human in the loop’; humans are a paramount linchpin in this cycle,” Lohani says. “If you don’t have governance and human oversight, it will fail.”</p><h3>​RESERVING THE RIGHT TO CLICK “BUY”</h3><br /><p>That’s also the prevailing wisdom in the retail sector, where a number of big players are using agentic AI to assist shoppers with their online purchases—say, by comparing prices, shipping times, and availability; sending them alerts on price drops for items they’ve viewed or added to their wish lists; and narrowing down options based on historical preferences.</p><p>However, there appear to be limits to how much control shoppers are willing to cede. <a href="https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2025/06/04/walmarts-retail-rewired-report-2025-agentic-ai-at-the-heart-of-retail-transformation" target="_blank">In a report released this summer,</a> megaretailer Walmart found that while consumers are increasingly open to letting AI guide their shopping journeys, they’re not yet willing to trust a digital assistant to choose and purchase products on their behalf<em>.</em></p><p>“There is a strong desire for human-in-the-loop systems where human oversight and control is maintained,” Desiree Gosby, Walmart’s senior vice president, tech strategy and emerging tech, said in <a href="https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2025/06/04/walmarts-retail-rewired-report-2025-agentic-ai-at-the-heart-of-retail-transformation" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">a release</a> announcing the findings of the study, <em>Walmart’s Retail Rewired Report 2025: Agentic AI at the Heart of Retail Transformation</em>. “In fact, 46% of respondents said they were either somewhat unlikely or very unlikely to use a digital assistant or agent to handle an entire shopping trip for them. To me, the preference is clear: AI can help guide, but shoppers want to be the ones clicking ‘buy.’”</p><p>Walmart concluded that shoppers are looking for ways to “keep the fun” of shopping while simplifying the practical parts with digital assistants.</p><p>Striking that balance is a clear goal throughout the industry, whether it’s on websites, in stores, or in warehouses and DCs, tech leaders say. During Covid, shippers and service providers went through an unprecedented period of disruption, where many forecasts weren’t worth the spreadsheets they were calculated on.</p><p>The Covid pandemic was the mother of all disruptions for freight carriers. Fast forward to today. The freight markets have regained a semblance of normalcy, even in the face of disruptive tariff and trade policies, cautious consumers, weakening job growth, shrinking U.S. factory output, and an economy that appears to be losing some steam. Shippers continue to navigate an environment of uncertainty and constant change.</p><p>That’s become the new normal for the nation’s rail and intermodal operators.</p><h4>DIFFERENT STRATEGIES</h4><p>“There are different strategies being used to plan for need based on size and scope of the client, the complexity of their supply chain, and the industry vertical they’re operating in,” observes Todd Davis, senior vice president of sales for intermodal service provider <a href="https://www.stgusa.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">STG Logistics</a>, which generates some $2 billion in revenues annually.</p><p>In his discussions with customers, he’s found that “the number-one struggle is forecasting,” driven mostly by uncertain tariff and trade policies, and their effect on trade flows, sourcing strategies, and costs.</p><p>“There is a lot of ‘whack a mole’ going on,” Davis observes, likening it to defeating one problem in one area only to have another pop up in a different area. Unlike the tariff situation in the spring, “this one has a lot more moving parts” that shippers have had to contend with, given the multiple tariff levels being applied, at different times, to different countries and commodities.</p><p>With all this at hand, “think of the team of supply chain planners inside a company, trying to secure capacity based on one forecast, only to have that forecast change a week later and then a week after that. All that ripples right down to the carrier,” Davis notes.</p><p>“We see a lot of start and stop, going through spikes and lulls,” he adds. “How does that influence pricing and costs? [Uncertainty] affects how we plan our network, position capacity, and operate to meet the client’s service needs—which are as exacting today as ever.”</p><p>Such a roller coaster of a market also demands resilience and agility, Davis emphasizes. “What we all want is that even keel [of demand] so we can [plan] our service for customers, ensure there’s capacity when and where they need it, and provide that predictability they expect.”</p><p>STG is the fourth-largest private intermodal asset owner in the U.S., deploying some 15,000 53-foot intermodal containers and 2,700 trucks and drivers performing drayage services for domestic as well as import/export boxes. It is also a leading LCL (less-than-containerload) consolidator and operates local and regional container freight stations throughout the U.S.</p><h4>NOT FOR THE FAINT-HEARTED</h4><p>Maintaining, building, and consistently operating a national rail network is not for the financially faint-hearted. It is perhaps the most capital-intensive of all the surface transportation sectors. In some cases, that means joining forces. Case in point: <a href="https://www.up.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Union Pacific</a>’s announcement of an $85 billion plan to acquire <a href="https://www.norfolksouthern.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Norfolk Southern</a>, which would create the first U.S. transcontinental railroad.</p><p>The new company would control rail routes across 43 states, connecting approximately 100 ports. The combination of Union Pacific’s western routes and Norfolk Southern’s eastern network would connect over 50,000 miles of track and could potentially yield billions in cost savings.</p><p>While some constituencies oppose the deal because, in their view, it would consolidate capacity in a negative way, reduce competition and jobs, put rail safety at risk, and increase costs, proponents argue the merger will improve supply chain efficiency, reliability, and transit times through economies of scale and streamlined operations.</p><p>“This merger is a win-win for our customers, our employees, and the American economy,” said a Union Pacific spokesperson. “We’ve spoken to hundreds of customers who are excited about faster and more reliable service, competitive single-line pricing, and end-to-end visibility across a transcontinental network—while protecting good union jobs.”</p><p>The spokesperson also cited the benefit of creating “safer, less-congested roadways through the conversion of truck traffic to rails.” The merger awaits the approval of the U.S. Surface Transportation Board.</p><p>Then there is the example of the <a href="https://www.bnsf.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Burlington Northern Santa Fe Railway</a> (BNSF). It has invested over $17 billion in its network over the past five years, and in 2025, will pour in another $3.8 billion.</p><p>“We continue to leverage our industry-leading capacity, including the largest intermodal facilities in North America, the only three capable of processing 1 million containers a year,” notes Zak Andersen, BNSF’s chief of staff and vice president of communications. BNSF expanded its key Southern Transcon route, which stretches 2,200 miles between Southern California and Chicago, and has nearly completed the project to double, triple, and quadruple track capacity. On its Northern Transcon route, it has added nearly 100 miles of double track, 12 new and extended sidings, and 1,200 miles of centralized track control. It also is building three new state-of-the-art logistics parks, in Phoenix, Denver, and Barstow, California.</p><p>These infrastructure investments are helping BNSF “stay ahead of demand and have the capacity needed to be more truck-like, all while driving significant supply chain savings to cargo shippers,” Andersen notes.</p><p>BNSF also in late August announced a new coast-to-coast direct domestic intermodal service product with fellow Class 1 railroad <a href="https://www.csx.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">CSX</a> that will bridge the two networks and provide customers with immediate transcontinental offerings. It encompasses intermodal service between Southern California; Charlotte, North Carolina; and Jacksonville, Florida. It’s also launching a new service between Phoenix and Atlanta “aiming to convert OTR [over-the-road] freight to rail,” the company said. Also coming online is a new direct international intermodal service that connects the Port of New York & New Jersey and Norfolk, Virginia, with Kansas City. The company expects to announce additional service enhancements soon.</p><p>All the initiatives are intended to help customers navigate a challenging market by providing more reliable, available, and consistent intermodal rail service across the board, Andersen says. “We have seen and heard a wide variety of strategies from our customers this year,” as they work through a laundry list of tariff-related and other disruptions to their business and their freight flows. Yet BNSF has persevered with a singular goal: offering solutions to customers, he says. “The capacity we have invested in allows us to flex up and deliver when our customers need us the most.”</p><p>The market for intra-North America north-south service has seen change as well. In the two years since the Canadian Pacific and Kansas City Southern railroads combined into a single company under the <a href="https://www.cpkcr.com/en" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">CPKC</a> name, it has become “the first and only single-line railway connecting Canada, the United States, and Mexico, positioning itself as a key player in North American rail transportation,” the company says.</p><p>Among its offerings are its Mexico Midwest Express service between Chicago and Mexico, “presenting a competitive alternative to trucking,” the company says. In cooperation with CSX, CPKC also has opened “an east-west Class 1 corridor” providing Southern U.S. shippers with expedited transit times, increased capacity, and more sustainable freight options between Canada, the U.S., and Mexico. Ottensmeyer International Railway Bridge in Laredo, Texas, which more than doubles the previous capacity of this critical trade gateway. On the other, there were some bright spots when those tariffs were eased.</p><p>“It was like the tale of two cities,” she recalls. And while “we love our trucks because we partner with them on the drayage side, we want to achieve the highest and best use for each mode,” Reinke says. <img src="https://www.dcvelocity.com/media-library/lynn-hediger-of-orbis-corp.jpg?id=61631215&amp;width=1200&amp;height=600&amp;coordinates=0%2C0%2C0%2C302" /><br /><br /><p>With a strong foundation in manufacturing and finance, Lynn Hediger brings a holistic approach to product and process innovation. Hediger has been with Orbis for more than 27 years in a variety of engineering and management positions. In his current role, he oversees multiple product management, marketing, and engineering functions, guiding cross-functional teams in new product design and market development. Hediger holds a master’s degree in engineering management from the Milwaukee School of Engineering.</p><p><strong>Q: How would you describe the current state of the plastic packaging industry?</strong></p><p><strong>A:</strong> The reusable packaging industry is undergoing a significant transformation, shaped by sustainability imperatives, regulatory shifts, technological innovation, automation advances, labor availability issues, and evolving customer expectations, which are leading to increased opportunities in this space. Today’s consumers are looking to buy from sustainable brands, and that starts with how their product is moved in the supply chain.</p><p><strong>Q: What are the main advantages of using plastic containers and pallets instead of corrugated cartons and wood pallets?</strong></p><p><strong>A:</strong> Sustainability comes to mind first. Reusable packaging can be made from recycled raw materials. It is reusable many times and is recyclable at the end of its useful life. Ultimately, companies want to move their product faster, more safely, more cost-effectively, and more sustainably, and reusable packaging helps drive these benefits.</p><p>Reusables are durable, hygienic, cleanable, ergonomic, safer (no dust, splinters, or nails), and dimensionally consistent, making them ideal for use by workers or automated systems. Being reusable also makes adding track-and-trace capabilities (such as RFID tags) for the packaging and contents feasible. All this leads to economic, operational, and environmental benefits when viewed over the lifecycle of the packaging.</p><p><strong>Q: Are plastic containers best suited for closed-loop systems, or can they also be utilized for other types of shipments? </strong></p><p><strong>A:</strong> Reusable packaging is an ideal choice for closed-loop systems. However, we do see reusables used in certain one-way applications where wood and paper corrugate will not suffice due to regulations (i.e., requiring a heat-treated pallet for international shipments) or performance issues (moisture impacting paper corrugate). There is also an existing and growing market of open-pooling companies that utilize reusable plastic packaging as part of their rental programs for one-way packaging.</p><p><strong>Q: What is the typical lifecycle of plastic containers, and what happens to them at the end of their lives?</strong></p><p><strong>A:</strong> This is highly variable based on how the packaging is handled. Our 40 x 48 Odyssey line of reusable pallets travel more than 400 trips in the supply chain compared to an average of 11 trips for wood pallets, according to testing done at Virginia Tech. With proper use, we typically see our products last hundreds of trips over a five- to 10-year life.</p><p>At the end of its useful life, the product can be recovered, recycled, and reprocessed into new packaging. Orbis offers customers our <a href="https://www.orbiscorporation.com/en-us/services/recycle-with-orbis/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Recycle with Orbis</a> program, where we give sales credits toward future packaging purchases for returning their used packaging to us to be recycled.</p><p><strong>Q: With the concerns over forever plastics, is Orbis looking at making containers from biodegradable materials?</strong></p><p> <strong>A:</strong> While biodegradable materials show promise in many applications, our focus is on building durable, recyclable solutions that support a truly circular economy. Given the long service life and load requirements of our products, biodegradable materials would present too great a risk of product failure over time as the materials begin to break down. At Orbis, we believe the most sustainable packaging is the one that stays in use.</p><p><strong>Q: What are the advantages of using plastic containers and pallets within automated systems?</strong></p><p><strong>A: </strong>There are several advantages, starting with dimensional consistency, which drives repeatable and reliable performance in these systems. There is no dust or debris to cause downtime or maintenance issues. Durability and strength allow for stacking, even when totes are only partially full, and for ease of conveying and handling over a long service life. Tracking technologies such as RFID [radio-frequency identification] tags and barcodes can be precisely placed so sensors and vision systems used by the automation are optimized. In the area of reusable racks to transport product, we are producing precision racks for highly automated assembly cells.</p><p><strong>Q: How do plastic containers help to better cube products in storage and transportation?</strong></p><p><strong>A:</strong> Reusable products are designed with cube efficiency in mind. For most customers, industry standard sizes such as the GMA 40 x 48 or the AIAG 45 x 48 footprints work well to optimize product in each tote, the number of totes on a pallet, and the number of pallets on a truck. Custom-sized containers for unique applications that still optimize cube requirements are also possible. Storage and return logistics of empty containers are also considered in design where containers can nest or collapse to increase the quantities on a truck or density in a warehouse.</p><p><strong>Q: What factors should companies consider when weighing a switch to reusable packaging?</strong></p><strong>A:</strong> Companies considering reusable packaging should look at the entire supply chain to understand how the product flows, evaluate their current packaging to identify areas for improvement, determine meaningful metrics to measure success, select the right product for the application, identify champions, and rely on [their] packaging supplier to support a seamless conversion to reusable packaging. They can also conduct lifecycle and return-on-investment (ROI) studies to justify the project, bearing in mind that flexible financing solutions are available to accelerate return on investment.
